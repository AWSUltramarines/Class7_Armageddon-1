# **Armageddon Labs 1–3 — Student Reflection & Submission README**

## **Overview**
Labs 1–3 in the Armageddon series were not traditional “follow the steps and get the answer” exercises. Instead, they were designed to shift how I think about systems, troubleshooting, and responsibility. These labs intentionally removed the comfort of constant confirmation and forced me to rely on observation, reasoning, and evidence.

This README summarizes what these labs were meant to teach, how I approached them, and what I learned while completing them.

---

## **Purpose of Labs 1–3**

### **Lab 1 — Learning to Reason About Infrastructure**
Lab 1 introduced the idea that infrastructure is something I must *understand*, not just configure. Instead of clicking through a console, I had to build components, connect them, and verify them using logs and evidence. When something didn’t work, the goal wasn’t to rush to a fix—it was to understand *why*.  
This lab taught me to slow down, observe system behavior, and confirm reality before making assumptions.

### **Lab 2 — Understanding Exposure and Impact**
Lab 2 expanded the scope. My systems were no longer isolated; traffic paths, access controls, and external interfaces mattered. Small configuration choices suddenly had bigger consequences.  
This lab taught me that security and reliability aren’t add‑ons—they’re built into the architecture itself. When the blast radius grows, so should my caution.

### **Lab 3 — Designing Within Real‑World Constraints**
Lab 3 introduced constraints that exist in real environments: data residency, regional boundaries, identity trust, and cross‑region communication. These aren’t optional—they’re requirements.  
This lab taught me that good engineering isn’t about avoiding constraints; it’s about designing responsibly within them.

Together, Labs 1–3 weren’t tests of intelligence—they were training in judgment. Feeling uncertain or slow wasn’t failure; it was part of learning how real systems behave.

---

## **What Was Expected of Me**

### **1. Reading Before Asking**
I learned that most answers were already in the documentation, logs, or system behavior. Reading first made my questions more precise and meaningful.

### **2. Working Through Uncertainty**
These labs didn’t give instant confirmation. I had to get comfortable operating without constant feedback. That discomfort pushed me to think instead of react.

### **3. Verifying Everything With Evidence**
Assumptions weren’t enough. I had to prove what existed, how components were connected, and how traffic flowed. Logs, CLI commands, and repeated checks became part of my workflow.

### **4. Slowing Down as Impact Increased**
As the labs progressed, the systems affected more components. One fix to a module can break something in another module. I also learned copy and paste is my friend.

### **5. Troubleshooting Without a Script**
When something broke, I wasn’t supposed to panic or ask for the answer. I was expected to gather information, form a hypothesis, test it, and adjust—just like real engineers do.

### **6. Asking Good Questions**
Questions within my group were encouraged, but only after I had done the work. The goal wasn’t shortcuts—it was clarity.

### **7. Finishing With Understanding, Not Perfection**
A working system mattered, but understanding *why* it worked mattered more. Partial success with clear reasoning was better than blind success.

### **8. Persisting Through Difficulty**
These labs required endurance. The expectation wasn’t perfection—it was engagement, reflection, and steady improvement.

---

## **What Was *Not* Expected of Me**

- I wasn’t expected to understand everything immediately.  
- I wasn’t expected to finish quickly.  
- I wasn’t expected to memorize commands or syntax.  
- I wasn’t expected to avoid mistakes.  
- I wasn’t expected to know “the right answer” on the first try.  
- I wasn’t expected to compare myself to others.  
- I wasn’t expected to be perfect.

The labs were designed so mistakes were safe and recoverable. The real goal was learning how to think, not how to follow instructions.

---

## **Final Reflection**
Labs 1–3 changed how I approach technical work. Instead of looking for the fastest path, I learned to look for the *correct* one. Instead of assuming, I learned to verify. Instead of rushing, I learned to slow down when the stakes increased. And instead of fearing uncertainty, I learned to work through it.

These labs were challenging, but they were also the most realistic introduction to how systems behave in the real world. The lessons I learned here will carry forward into every environment I build from now on.
